01

Before Formation — the decisions that shape everything else

Decide these before you spend a single dollar

Everything downstream — your taxes, your bank options, your ability to raise money later — is determined by choices you make now. Do not skip this part to "just get the company formed." A wrong entity or state choice is annoying and expensive to unwind.

Which state: Wyoming vs. Delaware vs. New Mexico

For two non-US founders bootstrapping a SaaS company that will not immediately raise venture capital, Wyoming is the right default. Here's the honest comparison.

◆ Why Wyoming

Lowest predictable running cost of any credible state ($60/yr), no state income or franchise tax, real member privacy, near-instant online formation, and a strong enough reputation that Wise, Payoneer, Stripe, and most fintechs onboard Wyoming LLCs without friction. Delaware's advantages (the Court of Chancery, investor familiarity) matter mostly to C-Corps raising VC — not to a bootstrapped LLC. New Mexico is cheaper on paper but its extreme anonymity gets it extra compliance scrutiny from banks; for a company that needs banking and Stripe, that trade-off is not worth $60/yr.

◷ Now or later

Choose now and don't overthink it. You can re-domesticate to Delaware later (Part 19) if you raise money. Starting in Wyoming costs you nothing if you convert.

LLC or C-Corp?

An LLC for now. A C-Corp is only worth its double taxation and heavier compliance if you are raising priced venture rounds or granting equity to US employees on standard terms. For two founders selling SaaS and keeping the profits, the LLC's pass-through treatment is simpler and cheaper.

◆ Why not a C-Corp yet

A C-Corp pays 21% federal corporate tax on profits, and then you're taxed again when money reaches you. As non-residents you'd also wade into dividend withholding. An LLC "passes through" — the company itself generally pays no federal income tax; the tax picture lands on the members. And if operations run from Bangladesh, that picture is often no US income tax at all (Part 13). Convert to a C-Corp the moment a real investor asks you to — not before.

Why a multi-member LLC (and not two single-member LLCs)

Because there are two of you and you want to own one business together, a single multi-member LLC is correct. Two single-member LLCs would mean two of everything (two EINs, two bank accounts, two Stripe accounts) and no clean shared ownership. One multi-member LLC gives you shared ownership, one bank account, one Stripe, and one tax return.

▲ Critical: this changes your tax forms

A multi-member foreign-owned LLC is taxed as a partnership and files Form 1065 + a Schedule K-1 per member + Schedules K-2/K-3. It does not file Form 5472 + pro-forma 1120 — that's the single-member rule. Filing the wrong form is the #1 fatal mistake foreign founders make. See Part 13.

Tax implications for two non-US founders (the short version)

Full detail is in Part 13, but decide with these facts in mind:

  • The LLC-partnership itself usually owes no US federal income tax.
  • Whether you owe US income tax depends on whether the company has Effectively Connected Income (ECI) — income from a US trade or business. If both of you do all the work from Bangladesh and you have no US employees/office/inventory, your SaaS revenue is generally not ECI, so generally no US income tax for you personally.
  • You must still file the partnership return every year even with zero US tax due. Filing is mandatory; tax may be $0.
  • The US and Bangladesh have no income tax treaty — so there's no treaty rate to fall back on. Confirm your Bangladesh (NBR) obligations locally.

Should both founders appear from day one?

Yes. If you both own the company, both should be members from formation. Adding a member later is possible but creates paperwork and, in edge cases, tax events. Put the real ownership on paper from the start.

How ownership percentages work

Ownership = "membership interest," expressed as a percentage or as "units." Two equal founders are usually 50/50, or 50 units each of 100. Ownership drives three things: share of profits, share of losses, and (unless you separate them) voting power.

◆ Example

Rahim and Karim each contribute equal work and a little cash. They set 50/50. Later a technical advisor joins for 10%; they each drop to 45%. All of this is defined in the Operating Agreement (Part 6), not the state filing.

✕ Common mistake

Doing a "handshake 50/50" and never writing it down. When one founder leaves, disappears, or dies, an undocumented split becomes a legal nightmare. Write it down (Parts 6–7).

How voting rights work

By default voting usually tracks ownership (50/50 = a possible deadlock). You can decouple them: e.g., each founder gets one vote regardless of %, or certain "major decisions" (selling the company, taking on debt, admitting a member) require unanimous consent while day-to-day decisions need a simple majority. Decide and record a deadlock-breaker now — this is the single most valuable clause for a two-person company.

What to decide before forming

  • Company name (and a backup)
  • Ownership split and whether it can change (vesting?)
  • Who is the "Responsible Party" for the IRS (one of you)
  • Voting rules + deadlock-breaker
  • What happens if a founder leaves, dies, or wants out (buyback)
  • That all code/IP belongs to the LLC, not to either of you personally (Part 8)

The Organizer is just whoever signs the formation document — a clerical role that ends once the LLC exists. It does not have to be an owner. Ownership is defined separately in your Operating Agreement. Many RA services list themselves as Organizer; that's normal and fine.

Registering the LLC Yourself on the Wyoming Portal

wyobiz.wyo.gov · ~15 minutes · $100 + ~2.4% card fee

Legally required
Yes
Cost
$100 + ~$2.40 card fee
Time
Online: near-instant
Do it
First

You'll file Articles of Organization on the Wyoming Secretary of State's WyoBiz portal. This is the document that legally creates the company. Below is the flow page-by-page. Wyoming occasionally tweaks the portal, so treat this as the meaning of each screen rather than pixel-exact instructions.

Step 0 — Check the name is available

On wyobiz.wyo.gov use the business name search. Your name must be unique and end with "LLC" or "L.L.C." Avoid restricted words (bank, insurance, etc.). You don't need to pay to "reserve" a name — availability is confirmed when your filing is approved.

Step 1 — Start the filing

Choose Limited Liability Company → Articles of Organization. Select a domestic LLC (formed in Wyoming), not "foreign" (which means an out-of-state company registering into WY).

Step 2 — Company name

Enter it exactly, including "LLC." This is your one chance to get the spelling permanent, so match what you'll use on the EIN and bank.

Step 3 — Registered Agent

Enter your commercial RA's name and Wyoming address (Part 4). If your RA service is filing for you, they populate this. You cannot leave it blank and you cannot use a Bangladesh address here — it must be a physical Wyoming address.

Step 4 — Principal office & mailing address

Principal office can be your real address (Bangladesh) or a US virtual address (Part 9). Mailing address is where the state sends paper — your RA or virtual US address is ideal.

Step 5 — Organizer

Name and sign as Organizer (you), or your RA if their package includes it. Wyoming does not publicly list members, so your names as owners won't appear on the public record — only the Organizer and RA do.

Step 6 — Consent of Registered Agent

Wyoming requires the RA to consent. A commercial RA handles this automatically; if you somehow act as your own agent (you can't from Bangladesh — you need a physical WY presence), you'd sign consent yourself.

Step 7 — Review, pay, submit

Pay the $100 filing fee by card (a ~2.4% processing fee applies online, roughly $2.40). Online filings are typically approved within minutes to a few hours. Download the stamped Articles of Organization and your filing ID — you'll need these PDFs for the EIN and the bank.

✕ Common mistakes on this page
  • Filing "Foreign LLC" instead of "Domestic" (creates the wrong entity).
  • Typos in the company name that then don't match your EIN.
  • Forgetting to save the approved PDF — banks want the stamped copy, not a screenshot.
  • Noting the wrong anniversary month: your annual report is forever due the 1st of the month you formed in. Forming near a month boundary (e.g. Jan 31) can be confusing — form early in a month you'll remember.
◆ Alternative

Most RA services (Northwest, etc.) will file the Articles for you as part of a formation package for ~$0–$100 over the state fee. If DIY-ing the portal feels risky, this is the one step where paying a little is defensible — but it's genuinely easy to do yourself.

A Wyoming physical address that receives your legal mail

Legally required
Yes
Cost
$25–$200 / yr
Time
Minutes to sign up

◆ Why it's required

Every Wyoming LLC must have an agent with a physical street address in Wyoming, available during business hours, to receive lawsuits ("service of process") and official state mail. Since you live in Bangladesh, you cannot be your own agent — you must hire a commercial one.

Good providers

  • Northwest Registered Agent — the reliability benchmark; strong privacy, good scanning/mail forwarding, human support. ~$125/yr.
  • Wyoming Registered Agent Services LLC / Cloud Peak Law — WY-local, founder-friendly.

Cheapest

  • Wyoming Agents / small local agents — as low as $25–$50/yr. Fine for the legal requirement; verify they scan/forward mail, which you'll rely on.
◷ Now or later

Now — you need the RA before filing (Step 3 asks for it). Pick one, get the WY address, then file.

▲ If it expires

If your RA lapses, Wyoming can administratively dissolve your LLC and reinstatement costs $350 (no-agent reinstatement) plus back fees. Autopay your RA and treat the renewal like rent. You can switch agents anytime by filing a change of agent (~$0–$5) — you are not locked in.

✕ Common mistake

Assuming the RA address is your "business address." It is not usable as your bank/Stripe business address (Parts 9 & 11). It's only for legal service and state mail.

EIN — Your Federal Tax ID, Without an SSN

The number that unlocks banking, Stripe, and tax filing

Legally required
Yes (foreign-owned)
Cost
$0 (free from IRS)
Time
Fax ~4 biz days–weeks
Do it
Right after formation

The EIN is your LLC's federal tax ID. You cannot open a bank account, activate Stripe, or file taxes without it. Getting one with an SSN is instant online — but you don't have an SSN, so you're on the paper track. That's completely normal and works reliably.

▲ The online tool will not work for you

IRS's online EIN application requires the responsible party to have an SSN or ITIN. Do not waste hours on it. Use fax or phone. The EIN itself is always free — anyone charging you is charging for convenience, not the number.

Method A — Fax (recommended for most)

  1. Download Form SS-4 from IRS.gov and fill every field (see line notes below).
  2. Fax the completed SS-4 (plus a passport copy of the responsible party helps) to the IRS EIN Operation: 855-641-6935 (or 855-215-1627). Fax runs 24/7.
  3. The IRS faxes back your EIN, typically in ~4 business days (sometimes longer during busy periods). This fax-back is usable immediately for banking and Stripe.
  4. The official CP-575 confirmation letter arrives separately by mail in 4–6 weeks to your RA/US address. If it never comes, request a 147C letter later.

No fax machine? Use an online fax service (HelloFax, FaxZero, etc.) — the IRS receives it identically.

Method B — Phone (international applicants line)

Call the IRS international EIN line at +1-267-941-1099 (Mon–Fri, ~6:00 AM–11:00 PM ET; this is not toll-free). Have your completed SS-4 in front of you — the officer reads it to you line by line and issues the EIN on the call in ~15–40 minutes. Call early morning ET for the shortest wait. Write the EIN down the moment they say it.

Method C — Mail (slowest, backup only)

Mail SS-4 to: Internal Revenue Service, Attn: EIN International Operation, Cincinnati, OH 45999, USA. Weeks to over a month. Use only if fax and phone both fail.

SS-4 line notes that trip people up

LineWhat to put
1Exact LLC legal name (matches Articles)
4a/4bMailing address (your RA/US address or Bangladesh)
7aResponsible party = one founder's full legal name
7bWrite "Foreign" (you have no SSN/ITIN). Do not leave blank.
8aYes, it's an LLC
8bNumber of members: 2
9aCheck "Partnership" (a 2-member LLC's default federal class)
10Reason: "Started new business" (or "Banking purpose")
11Date business started (your formation date)
12Closing month of accounting year: December
13Employees expected: usually 0
18"Has the entity ever applied for an EIN before?" — No
✕ Common mistakes
  • Trying the online tool for hours before discovering it needs an SSN.
  • Leaving line 7b blank instead of writing "Foreign."
  • Letting a formation agent list themselves as responsible party — this puts a stranger on your IRS record. The responsible party should be one of you.
  • Applying for a second EIN because the first was slow — use one method only, per entity.
  • Choosing the wrong entity class (don't accidentally elect corporation).
◷ Later admin note

If you ever change the responsible party or address, file Form 8822-B — within 60 days for a responsible-party change.

Operating Agreement

The internal rulebook that actually governs your company

State-required?
No (but essential)
Cost
$0 DIY / $200–$1k lawyer
Time
1–2 hrs to adapt
Do it
Right after formation
◆ Why you need one even though Wyoming doesn't require it

The Operating Agreement (OA) is the contract between the two of you. It proves who owns what, how decisions get made, and what happens when things go wrong. Banks and Stripe often ask for it. Without one, you fall back on Wyoming's default rules — which may not match your intentions and offer no deadlock-breaker. For two founders, this document is your relationship insurance.

A solid multi-member OA covers: ownership & units, capital contributions, profit/loss allocation and distributions, management & voting, major-decision list, transfer restrictions, member departure/removal, death/disability, buy-back mechanics, vesting, dispute resolution, dissolution, and IP ownership. Below is a professional starting template — adapt it, then have a US attorney glance over it before you rely on it for anything serious.

📄 Template — Multi-Member LLC Operating Agreement (starter)▸ 
OPERATING AGREEMENT OF [COMPANY NAME] LLC
A Wyoming Limited Liability Company

This Operating Agreement ("Agreement") is entered into and effective as of
[DATE], by and among the Members listed in Exhibit A (each a "Member" and
collectively the "Members") of [COMPANY NAME] LLC (the "Company").

1. FORMATION
1.1 The Company was formed under the Wyoming Limited Liability Company Act by
    filing Articles of Organization on [FORMATION DATE].
1.2 Principal office: [ADDRESS]. Registered agent: [RA NAME/ADDRESS].
1.3 Term: perpetual, until dissolved under this Agreement.

2. PURPOSE
2.1 To develop, market, and sell software-as-a-service products and to engage
    in any lawful business permitted to a Wyoming LLC.

3. MEMBERS, UNITS, AND OWNERSHIP
3.1 Membership interests are denominated in Units. Total issued Units and each
    Member's Units and Percentage Interest are set out in Exhibit A.
    (Example: [FOUNDER 1] — 50 Units — 50%; [FOUNDER 2] — 50 Units — 50%.)
3.2 New Units may be issued only by unanimous written consent of the Members.

4. CAPITAL CONTRIBUTIONS
4.1 Initial contributions (cash, code, IP, or services) are listed in Exhibit A.
4.2 No Member is required to make additional contributions. Any additional
    capital call requires unanimous consent; a Member who declines may be
    subject to dilution as set out in Exhibit B.

5. ALLOCATIONS AND DISTRIBUTIONS
5.1 Profits and losses are allocated to Members in proportion to Percentage
    Interests.
5.2 Distributions are made when and as approved by the Members, pro rata to
    Percentage Interests, only if the Company remains solvent afterward.
5.3 Tax withholding: the Company may withhold and remit any US tax required on a
    Member's allocable share (e.g., under IRC Section 1446) and treat it as a
    distribution to that Member.

6. MANAGEMENT AND VOTING
6.1 The Company is Member-managed.
6.2 Ordinary business decisions require a majority of Percentage Interests.
6.3 "Major Decisions" require UNANIMOUS consent: selling the Company or
    substantially all assets; taking on debt over $[AMOUNT]; admitting or
    removing a Member; amending this Agreement; dissolving the Company;
    changing tax classification; distributing or encumbering Company IP.
6.4 DEADLOCK: If the Members cannot agree on a Major Decision within [30] days,
    the matter goes to mediation (Section 12); if unresolved after [60] days,
    either Member may invoke the buy/sell ("shotgun") procedure in Exhibit B.

7. TRANSFER OF INTERESTS
7.1 No Member may sell, pledge, or transfer Units without the written consent
    of the other Member(s), except as permitted in this Agreement.
7.2 Right of First Refusal: a Member wishing to sell must first offer the Units
    to the other Member(s) on the same terms.

8. VESTING (recommended for founders)
8.1 Each founder's Units vest over [4] years with a [1]-year cliff, measured
    from [START DATE]. Unvested Units are subject to repurchase at cost (or par)
    if that founder ceases to actively work for the Company ("Departure").
8.2 Fully vested Units are not subject to forfeiture but remain subject to the
    transfer restrictions and buy-back terms of this Agreement.

9. DEPARTURE, REMOVAL, DEATH, AND DISABILITY
9.1 Voluntary departure: a departing Member must offer their Units to the
    Company/remaining Member(s) under the buy-back terms in Exhibit B.
9.2 Removal for cause (fraud, material breach, felony, abandonment of duties
    for [90]+ days): by vote of the other Member(s); removed Member's Units are
    subject to buy-back at the "for cause" price in Exhibit B.
9.3 Death or permanent disability: the Company/remaining Member(s) have the
    option to buy the affected Member's Units from them or their estate at the
    "fair value" price in Exhibit B, payable over [24] months.

10. BOOKS, RECORDS, AND TAX
10.1 The Company keeps accurate books; each Member may inspect them.
10.2 The Company files IRS Form 1065 and issues Schedule K-1 (and K-2/K-3) to
     each Member annually. [MEMBER NAME] is designated the Partnership
     Representative under the centralized partnership audit rules.
10.3 Fiscal year ends December 31.

11. INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
11.1 All software, source code, designs, trademarks, domains, and other IP
     created for the Company's business is the exclusive property of the
     Company. Each Member hereby assigns to the Company all such IP and will
     sign further documents to confirm the assignment (see separate IP
     Assignment Agreements).

12. DISPUTE RESOLUTION
12.1 Good-faith negotiation, then mediation in [Wyoming / neutral venue].
12.2 Unresolved disputes: binding arbitration under [AAA] rules; governing law
     is Wyoming. Prevailing party may recover reasonable fees.

13. DISSOLUTION
13.1 The Company dissolves on unanimous consent or as required by law. Assets
     are applied to creditors first, then distributed to Members pro rata.

14. GENERAL
14.1 This Agreement is the entire agreement among the Members and may be amended
     only in writing signed by all Members (subject to Section 6.3).
14.2 If any provision is unenforceable, the rest remains in effect.

IN WITNESS WHEREOF, the Members have executed this Agreement as of the date
first written above.

_______________________     _______________________
[FOUNDER 1], Member          [FOUNDER 2], Member

EXHIBIT A — Members, Units, Contributions
EXHIBIT B — Buy-Back Prices & Buy/Sell (Shotgun) Mechanics
✕ Common mistakes

Skipping the OA "because it's just the two of us"; omitting a deadlock-breaker on a 50/50 split; forgetting vesting (so a founder who quits in month 2 keeps 50% forever); and not naming a Partnership Representative for IRS audits.

07

Founders Agreement

Do you also need one? Usually the OA covers it — but here's when it helps

For a two-person LLC, a well-drafted Operating Agreement (Part 6) already contains the substance of a founders agreement: ownership, vesting, roles, departure, and IP. A separate Founders Agreement is worth adding when you want to spell out working expectations that don't belong in a formal OA — time commitment, roles, salaries, side projects, and confidentiality — in plainer language you'll both actually read.

◷ Recommendation

Sign the OA as your binding legal document, and add this short Founders Agreement as the "how we'll actually work together" companion. Both signed the same week.

📄 Template — Founders Agreement (starter)▸ 
FOUNDERS AGREEMENT — [COMPANY NAME] LLC
Between [FOUNDER 1] and [FOUNDER 2] ("Founders"), dated [DATE].

1. PURPOSE
   We are building [COMPANY / PRODUCT DESCRIPTION] together and want a clear,
   shared understanding of ownership, roles, and expectations.

2. OWNERSHIP
   Ownership is [50% / 50%] as reflected in the Operating Agreement, subject to
   the vesting schedule below.

3. VESTING
   Each Founder's ownership vests over [4] years with a [1]-year cliff from
   [START DATE]. If a Founder leaves before vesting, unvested interest returns
   to the Company as set out in the Operating Agreement.

4. ROLES & TIME COMMITMENT
   [FOUNDER 1]: [e.g., product & backend]. Full-time from [DATE].
   [FOUNDER 2]: [e.g., growth & frontend]. Full-time from [DATE].
   Each Founder will devote substantially full working time to the Company and
   will not take on competing work without written consent.

5. DECISIONS
   Day-to-day: each Founder leads their own area. Major decisions (as defined in
   the Operating Agreement) require agreement of both.

6. COMPENSATION
   Until the Company can pay salaries, Founders take $[0] salary. Distributions
   are made only when approved and only if the Company stays solvent.

7. INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY & CONFIDENTIALITY
   All work product and IP belongs to the Company (see IP Assignment). Each
   Founder keeps Company information confidential during and after involvement.

8. DEPARTURE
   A Founder who leaves follows the buy-back and transfer terms of the Operating
   Agreement. We agree to act in good faith and give [30] days' notice.

9. DISPUTES
   We will attempt to resolve disagreements directly, then by mediation, before
   any formal action, consistent with the Operating Agreement.

Signed:
_______________________     _______________________
[FOUNDER 1]                  [FOUNDER 2]

IP Assignment — Put the Code in the Company

So the LLC owns the product, not either founder personally

◆ Why this matters more than founders expect

By default, code a person writes can belong to that person, not the company — even a founder. If your IP isn't formally assigned to the LLC, then a departing co-founder could theoretically claim they still own half the codebase, and any future investor or acquirer will refuse to proceed until IP ownership is clean. Fix it early with a one-page assignment from each founder to the company.

Have each founder sign an IP Assignment to the LLC, and use a Contractor IP Assignment for anyone you hire (freelancers, designers). Also register the LLC as owner of your domains and accounts.

📄 Template — Founder IP Assignment▸ 
INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY ASSIGNMENT AGREEMENT

This Agreement is made on [DATE] between [FOUNDER NAME] ("Assignor") and
[COMPANY NAME] LLC ("Company").

1. ASSIGNMENT. Assignor irrevocably assigns to the Company all right, title, and
   interest, worldwide, in and to all inventions, software, source code, object
   code, designs, documentation, trademarks, domain names, know-how, and other
   intellectual property that Assignor has created or will create relating to
   the Company's business (collectively, the "IP"), including all copyrights,
   patents, trade secrets, and moral rights (waived to the extent permitted).

2. PRIOR WORKS. Any pre-existing IP that Assignor incorporates into Company
   products is listed in Schedule 1; for such items Assignor grants the Company
   a perpetual, worldwide, royalty-free, irrevocable license.

3. FURTHER ASSURANCES. Assignor will sign any documents and take any actions the
   Company reasonably requests to perfect, register, or enforce the IP, and
   appoints the Company as attorney-in-fact to do so if Assignor is unavailable.

4. CONSIDERATION. Assignor makes this assignment in consideration of Assignor's
   membership interest in and continued relationship with the Company.

5. GOVERNING LAW. Wyoming, USA.

_______________________          _______________________
[FOUNDER NAME], Assignor          For [COMPANY NAME] LLC

SCHEDULE 1 — Prior Works (if any):
📄 Template — Contractor / Freelancer IP Assignment clause▸ 
WORK-FOR-HIRE & IP ASSIGNMENT (for contractors)

All deliverables and work product created by Contractor for [COMPANY NAME] LLC
under this engagement are "works made for hire" and are the exclusive property

▲ The address that gets applications rejected

Since 2025, Mercury and most fintechs will not accept your registered agent's address as your business address, and they can detect RA/P.O.-box/UPS-store addresses. Give them a real street operating address — either your genuine Bangladesh address or a proper virtual office.

Good virtual address providers

  • iPostal1, Anytime Mailbox, Stable, Earth Class Mail — real street addresses with a unique suite, mail scanning and forwarding, ~$10–$30/mo.
✕ Never do this
  • Use your RA address as the business/bank address.
  • Invent an address you don't control.
  • Use a P.O. box or a bare UPS-Store box (flagged).
  • Mismatch the address across Articles, EIN, bank, and Stripe.
◷ Now or later

Set up the virtual address before the bank/Stripe step. You don't strictly need it to form the LLC or get the EIN (your BD address works there), but you'll want it ready for banking.

Stripe & Polar — Getting Paid

Two different models; for SaaS you may want both

Stripe (payment processor)

Stripe is the standard for accepting cards. With a US LLC + EIN + a US bank/Wise account + a real website, you can activate Stripe (US) directly — you do not need Stripe Atlas (that's just their formation product; you've already formed the company).

Stripe needs: LLC name + EIN, business address, bank/Wise details for payouts, your ID, and a live website that clearly describes what you sell and links to your policies.

Polar (Merchant of Record)

Polar (like Paddle or Lemon Squeezy) acts as the merchant of record — it sells your software to the customer and pays you. Crucially, it handles global sales tax and VAT for you, which removes a large compliance headache for a global SaaS. The trade-off is a higher fee than raw Stripe.

◆ Which to use

Many bootstrapped SaaS founders start on a Merchant of Record (Polar/Paddle) to offload worldwide tax collection, then add raw Stripe later for lower fees once volume justifies handling tax themselves. For two non-US founders who don't want to track sales-tax rules in dozens of jurisdictions, starting with a MoR is a very reasonable default.

Website requirements & required policies

Processors and MoRs require your site to have real, matching business info and these documents:

  • Terms of Service — the rules of using your product.
  • Privacy Policy — what data you collect and how (needed for GDPR too, since you'll have EU users).
  • Refund / Cancellation Policy — clear and reasonable; processors specifically look for this.
  • Clear pricing, a contact method, and a description matching your application.
◆ Example — a strong business description

Weak: "We provide online software solutions."
Strong: "We sell a subscription SaaS that helps small law firms automate client intake, billed monthly at $29–$99 via card. Customers are businesses in the US, EU, and Asia."

✕ Common mistakes
  • Applying with a coming-soon page — processors want a real, working site.
  • Copy-pasting someone else's ToS with their company name still in it.
  • No refund policy (a frequent hold/reject reason).
  • Description on the site not matching what you told the processor.
◷ Now or later

Last — after LLC, EIN, address, and bank/Wise. This is the final gate before money flows.

Taxes — In Detail

The part where a one-time CPA consult pays for itself

▲ The single most important tax fact for you

A multi-member foreign-owned LLC is a partnership for US tax. It files Form 1065 + Schedule K-1 (one per member) + Schedules K-2 & K-3. It does NOT file Form 5472 + pro-forma 1120 — that rule is only for single-member foreign-owned LLCs. Filing the wrong form (or none) triggers penalties. This alone is why so many two-founder foreign LLCs get in trouble.

Do you personally owe US income tax? It depends on "ECI"

US tax on a foreign partner turns on Effectively Connected Income (ECI) — income from a US trade or business.

  • If both founders do all the work from Bangladesh, with no US employees, office, agents, or inventory, your SaaS revenue is generally treated as foreign-source, not ECI → generally no US federal income tax for the partnership or for you personally, and no §1446 withholding.
  • If you create a US nexus (US employee, US office, US-based servers you control, a US-based partner actively running things), some income may become ECI → the partnership must withhold US tax on your share (Forms 8804 / 8805 / 8813, ~37% for individuals) and each of you files Form 1040-NR.
◆ Why you still file even with $0 tax

"No tax due" is not "no filing due." A partnership with the facts above still must file Form 1065 + K-1s + K-2/K-3 every year. The penalty for a late 1065 is roughly $255 per partner per month (up to 12 months) — so a two-member LLC three months late owes about $1,530 even with zero income. Filing on time is the cheapest thing you'll do all year.

Federal obligations checklist

  • Form 1065 (US Return of Partnership Income) — annually.
  • Schedule K-1 — one per member, showing each member's share.
  • Schedules K-2 & K-3 — required because you have foreign partners / international items.
  • Forms 8804 / 8805 / 8813 — only if there's ECI allocable to you (withholding).
  • Form 1040-NR — each foreign member, only if you have US-taxable income or need to claim withholding back.

Wyoming (state) obligations

Wyoming has no state income tax and no franchise tax. Your only recurring state item is the Annual Report ($60 minimum, due the 1st of your formation-anniversary month — see Part 14).

Sales tax / VAT

US "sales tax" on SaaS varies by state (some tax SaaS, some don't) and only applies once you cross a state's economic nexus threshold (commonly ~$100k in sales or 200 transactions into that state). Tracking this across 45+ states is a real burden — which is exactly why using a Merchant of Record (Polar/Paddle) is attractive: they collect and remit sales tax/VAT for you. If you use raw Stripe at scale, you'll need a sales-tax tool (Stripe Tax, Anrok, TaxJar) or a CPA.

Estimated taxes & franchise tax

No Wyoming franchise tax. Federal estimated taxes generally don't apply to you personally if you have no US-taxable income; if you develop ECI, quarterly §1446 withholding deposits (Form 8813) come into play — a CPA will set this up.

Does Bangladesh tax this?

Very possibly — you are tax residents of Bangladesh, and Bangladesh taxes residents. Income you draw from the US company (and potentially your share of company profits) may be taxable under NBR rules, and there is no US–Bangladesh tax treaty to reduce or coordinate the two systems. Talk to a Bangladeshi tax professional about how to report foreign business income and remittances. Don't assume "US company = invisible to NBR."

◷ CPA recommendation

Hire a US CPA/EA who explicitly lists foreign-owned partnership (1065 + K-2/K-3) experience. Specialist firms exist for exactly this (search "foreign-owned multi-member LLC 1065"). Budget roughly $300–$1,200/yr for the return. This is not the place to save money by guessing.

✕ Fatal mistakes
  • Filing Form 5472 (single-member rule) instead of 1065 (your rule).
  • Skipping the return because "we owe $0" — the penalty is per partner per month.
  • Omitting K-2/K-3 (IRS treats the return as incomplete).
  • Ignoring Bangladesh-side taxes entirely.

50+ Mistakes Foreign Founders Make

And exactly how to avoid each one

  1. Filing Form 5472 instead of Form 1065. That's the single-member rule; you're a partnership. → File 1065 + K-1 + K-2/K-3.
  2. Not filing at all because you owe $0. Penalty is ~$255/partner/month. → Always file, even at a loss.
  3. Omitting Schedules K-2/K-3. IRS treats the return as incomplete. → Include them.
  4. Planning around Mercury while living in Bangladesh. BD is prohibited. → Use Wise Business.
  5. Relying on Relay. It now needs an SSN/ITIN. → Wise/Payoneer.
  6. Using the RA address as your bank/business address. Auto-reject. → Real street/virtual address.
  7. Different name spellings across Articles/EIN/bank. → Lock one exact spelling and reuse it.
  8. Trying the online EIN tool without an SSN. → Fax or phone SS-4.
  9. Leaving SS-4 line 7b blank. → Write "Foreign."
  10. Letting a formation agent be the responsible party. → List a founder.
  11. Applying for two EINs when the first is slow. → One method, one entity.
  12. Skipping the Operating Agreement. → Always sign one, even for two people.
  13. 50/50 with no deadlock-breaker. → Add mediation + buy/sell clause.
  14. No vesting. A founder who quits keeps their full stake. → 4-yr vest, 1-yr cliff.
  15. Never assigning IP to the LLC. → Each founder signs an IP assignment.
  16. Contractors keep IP rights. → Work-for-hire + assignment in every contract.
  17. Choosing New Mexico for anonymity, then failing bank KYC. → Wyoming balances privacy + acceptance.
  18. Choosing Delaware and paying $300/yr franchise tax for no benefit. → Wyoming unless raising VC.
  19. Forming a C-Corp too early. → LLC now; convert when an investor requires it.
  20. Two single-member LLCs instead of one multi-member. → One shared entity.
  21. Letting the registered agent lapse. Dissolution + $350. → Autopay.
  22. Missing the WY annual report (1st of anniversary month). → Calendar it; WY reminders aren't enough.
  23. Missing March 15 for the 1065. → File or extend with Form 7004 by March 15.
  24. Assuming no Bangladesh tax. → Consult NBR/local pro; no US–BD treaty.
  25. Ignoring sales tax/VAT at scale. → Use a Merchant of Record or a tax tool.
  26. Applying to the bank before the EIN. → Wait for the EIN.
  27. Vague business description. "Online business" reads as risk. → Be specific.
  28. Free subdomain instead of your own domain. → Buy yourcompany.com.
  29. Website is a coming-soon page. → Launch a real site before Stripe.
  30. No refund policy. Common Stripe hold. → Publish ToS + Privacy + Refund.
  31. Copy-pasted policies with another company's name. → Customize them.
  32. Site description ≠ processor description. → Make them match.
  33. Reapplying after rejection. Often auto-rejected. → Get it right first time.
  34. P.O. box / bare UPS-Store box as address. Flagged. → Real virtual office suite.
  35. Burner phone number. Distrusted; SMS may fail. → Business VoIP with SMS.
  36. Mixing personal and business money. Pierces liability protection. → Separate accounts, always.
  37. No bookkeeping until tax time. → Track monthly with Wave/Xero.
  38. Losing the stamped Articles / CP-575. Banks want originals. → Save PDFs; request 147C if needed.
  39. Not reporting responsible-party changes (8822-B). → File within 60 days.
  40. Believing the EIN costs money. → It's free from the IRS.
  41. Overpaying agencies for what you can do yourself. → Use this manual; pay only where it saves real risk.
  42. Filing "Foreign LLC" on WyoBiz. That's for out-of-state entities. → Choose "Domestic."
  43. Forming on a month boundary and misjudging the anniversary. → Form early in a memorable month.
  44. Assuming US servers/employees are harmless. They can create ECI + US tax. → Understand nexus first.
  45. No Partnership Representative named. Needed for IRS audits. → Name one in the OA.

  53. Not consulting a CPA even once. The costliest mistake of all. → Budget one consult before tax season.

Future Growth

Multiple products, when to add an entity, and the VC path

One LLC can own many products, domains & brands

You do not need a new company per product. One LLC can run multiple SaaS products, own dozens of domains, and operate several brand names (register "doing business as"/DBA names or just market under different names). Everything shares one EIN, one bank account, one Stripe, and one tax return. This is the simplest and cheapest way to run a product studio.

◆ Example

Your LLC "Northlight Software LLC" runs InboxIQ, DocSort, and MetricBase — three products, three domains, one company, one 1065.

When to create a second LLC

  • Isolating risk/liability — a product with real legal exposure you want walled off.
  • Selling one product — a clean entity makes a single-product sale far easier.
  • Different ownership — a new product with a different partner or investor.
  • A "holding" structure — later, a parent holding company owning product subsidiaries.

Until one of those is true, keep it to one LLC. Extra entities mean extra RAs, EINs, returns, and cost.

When to convert to a Delaware C-Corp

Convert when you're raising priced venture capital or issuing standard employee stock options. VCs almost always require a Delaware C-Corp for its predictable law, share structures (preferred stock), and QSBS benefits. You can domesticate/convert the Wyoming LLC into a Delaware C-Corp when that day comes — plan it with a startup attorney, since it has tax consequences.

What happens if you raise VC

  • Flip to Delaware C-Corp; issue founder common stock (with vesting) and preferred stock to investors.
  • Set up a cap table (Carta/Pulley) and a stock option pool.
  • US tax and payroll complexity rises — you'll need a startup CPA and counsel.
  • Foreign-founder ownership is fine, but the corp itself becomes a US taxpayer (21% on profits) — a different world from the pass-through LLC.
◷ Bottom line

Stay a Wyoming multi-member LLC while bootstrapping. Add entities only for isolation or sale. Convert to Delaware C-Corp only when a real investor requires it — not "just in case."
